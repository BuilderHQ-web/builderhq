# Reference Research: how large platforms position themselves

Companion to `marketing-recon-brief.md`. Retrieved live on 10 August 2026. Every quote below came off the actual page unless marked otherwise. ## The fifteen patterns that signal scale

**1. Section count is the clearest maturity tell.** Felix 7, ProcurePro 7, BuildPass 8, Procore 8, Assignar 8, EstimateOne 9, Payapps 11, PlanRadar 14, Buildxact 15. Every enterprise-credible site sits at **seven to nine sections**. Length reads as anxiety; restraint reads as scale. A company that repeats itself is one that did not believe it was believed the first time.

**2. Category name in the H1, brand line in the subhead.** Procore: `Construction management software` / `Together, we can build it all`. Autodesk: eyebrow `CONSTRUCTION MANAGEMENT SOFTWARE` / `Build it with Autodesk`. Payapps: `CONSTRUCTION PROGRESS CLAIM SOFTWARE` / `Standardise, Streamline and Simplify Progress Claims`. Only a company that owns its category can afford a plain H1, so a plain H1 signals that you do.

**3. Every number carries a source, a date or a definition.** REA footnotes its audience figure to `Ipsos iris Online Audience Measurement Service, Jan 2025 - Nov 2025 (average), P14+, PC/laptop/smartphone/tablets...`. Xero daggers its "twice as fast" claim. Procore publishes its **3.7 Google Play score beside its three 4.5+ scores**. A number without provenance is decoration; the footnote itself is the signal.

**4. Penetration and tenure beat volume.** Payapps: `34 of Australia's Top 50 Builders` and `70% of the top 20 construction firms in Australia`. EstimateOne: `With over 17 years in Australian construction`. Buildsoft: `for over 40 years`. REA: `established in 1995 and is headquartered in Melbourne`. Master Builders: `Building Australia for more than 130 years`. Age, address and market share cannot be faked by a new entrant, which is exactly why they are worth stating.

**5. Unrounded telemetry outperforms rounded figures.** BuildPass publishes `1,547,594` forms submitted, `3,196,266` sign-ons, `9 days` average RFI close. Seven-digit unrounded counters read as a live readout from a working system; "10,000+ users" reads as a claim.

**6. Describe the industry first, then locate yourself inside it.** Master Builders Australia has **no hero headline and no hero CTA** — it opens on national statistics (`$337.3 billion in building and construction work`, `11.0% of GDP`, `1.38 million people`). ProcurePro's subheadline is an industry statistic, not a product claim: `90% of project spend is committed early, locking in risk and profit.` The scale of the problem transfers to whoever names it.

**7. Audiences get separate destinations, never a toggle.** REA splits by whole site. Xero splits by top-level nav item (`Small Business` / `Accountants & Bookkeepers`) with one dignified in-page doorway for the secondary audience. EstimateOne makes the three roles the primary nav: `Head Contractor | Subcontractor | Supplier`. Procore makes `Who We Serve` a peer of `Solutions`. **Not one serious site in this sweep used an "I am a…" role switcher.** A toggle says "we could not decide"; navigation says "we serve three markets".

**8. Modules group by lifecycle phase, not by product name.** Buildxact: Manage Leads → Win More Work → Manage the Build → Track Finances. Autodesk groups by workflow. Bluebeam runs a separate workflow axis entirely (Drawing Management, Design Review, Takeoffs, RFIs & Submittals, Handover). Buyers think in project stages.

**9. The homepage names the modules; the module pages prove them.** Stripe's homepage never enumerates the suite. Felix gives four cards with one dense paragraph each and a link out. Depth lives one level down, which is also why serious sites can afford short homepages.

**10. Testimonials state facts, not feelings.** The Felix format is the gold standard, verbatim: *"We've been using Felix since 2017, and today, Felix is deeply embedded in our operations. We have over 250 users actively managing our network of 5,000+ vendors through the platform."* — James Davies, Chief Operating Officer, Abergeldie. **Customer since [year] + [N] users + [N] records under management + full name + exact title + company.** It cannot be faked and it reads as an audited fact rather than a compliment.

**11. Trust is a section, not an adjective.** Docusign runs `Trusted the world over` with a named certification list (ISO 27001, FedRAMP, PCI DSS…), a Trust Center, a System Status link and a Legality Guide. Harvey lists SOC2 II / ISO 27001 / ISO 27701 / ISO 42001 and regional data centres including AU. You publish the artefacts rather than claiming the adjective.

**12. Restraint in the CTA set.** BuildPass has three CTAs on the whole page. **Xero's hero has exactly one button and it goes to the price list**, not to a signup. Assignar uses four different wordings of the same demo request on one page and reads less confident for it.

**13. Typography does the work decoration usually does.** Xero: roughly 60px hero, sentence case, tight leading, half-width measure, one headline plus one sentence occupying a full viewport. Linear: one variable typeface in a narrow 300–590 weight band, -0.022em tracking, **0.5px hairline borders instead of shadows**. Quiet pages, precise type, no default values.

**14. Real product UI at real fidelity.** Procore shows a named project dashboard with plausible data. Xero composites genuine interface chrome into editorial photography (a baker at a floured bench, a floating card reading `Recent invoices / $2,260 / Paid`). Nobody serious uses a generic dashboard illustration. EstimateOne goes further and publishes **live tender inventory** on a public marketing page, with location, value band and category — a liquidity proof no screenshot can match.

**15. Australian spelling, rationed vernacular.** Felix, Payapps, Procore AU and Xero use "minimise", "standardise", "organisation" throughout. EstimateOne says "Subbies" only where it addresses a subcontractor directly and "Subcontractors" everywhere formal. Felix and ProcurePro use no vernacular at all.

## The fifteen patterns to avoid

1. **A hero that is a form instead of a position.** hipages: `Connect with trusted tradies`, then straight into "What do you need help with?" and a Go button. Nothing above the fold says what the company is, who runs it, or since when. The defining move of lead generation.
2. **The conversion verb as a permanent nav item.** hipages carries "Get quotes" in the masthead. Serious platforms put the thing itself in the nav: Investors, Pricing, Who We Serve.
3. **Throughput statistics dressed as proof.** "A new job posted every 25 seconds" measures conveyor speed and functions as soft urgency.
4. **Volume promised to both sides with no quality commitment.** Fatal for a tendering platform whose argument is that comparability beats quote volume.
5. **Income-claim testimonials.** "$3 million in contracts with more in pipeline" is franchise-pitch register.
6. **SEO furniture on the front door.** Bracketed year stamps in headings, city-by-trade link grids, "Cost Guides" in the nav. Programmatic pages should exist; they should not be furniture on the homepage.
7. **Fifteen sections saying the same thing three times.** Buildxact runs its three core capabilities twice on one page.
8. **Title-Case-Every-Word headings and slogan fragments.** "Less Juggling. More Building.", "Win Back Your Day", "Your Smartest Crew Member Yet". Sentence case and complete sentences read older and more serious.
9. **Exclamation marks and shouty caps.** Archistar closes an otherwise credible page with "Try AI powered property research, FREE!". One exclamation mark undoes a page of restraint.
10. **Inconsistent CTA wording.**
11. **Page-builder tells.** Three competing typefaces on one page, inconsistent section padding, drop-shadowed cards, gradient icon circles, stock hard-hat photography.
12. **Transformational AI language.** The credible framing is flat and operational — Harvey: *"Purpose built agents execute complex legal work end to end"*. AI described as a revolution rather than a step in a process is the tell of an AI-written site. For BuilderHQ, "every page read" is a factual claim about a procedure and must stay one.
13. **Institutional facts hidden on a second domain.** hipages' ASX listing, annual report and market analysis all exist, one domain away, invisible to every customer.
14. **A hero that has stopped selling.** Databuild's homepage hero at retrieval was `Update your Databuild software version!`.
15. **Trust reduced to two badges,** and "verified" used without ever defining what was checked.

## Two findings that are strategic openings

**The craft bar in Australian construction software is genuinely low.** Verified from the HTML: not one Australian construction platform in this sweep runs a bespoke, design-forward marketing site. EstimateOne is WordPress plus HubSpot on Roboto 400/700/**900**. Buildxact is WordPress plus Elementor with three competing typefaces. Felix has the best structure and tone in the category but standard-issue craft. A coherent type system with real editorial discipline would be **differentiating rather than merely competent**.

**Australian provenance is uncontested.** Aconex was founded in Melbourne in 2000 (the name contracts "Australian Construction Exchange"), listed on the ASX, and sold to Oracle for roughly A$1.6bn in 2017 — and Oracle's marketing retains **none** of that origin. Procore and Autodesk localise logos and nothing else. Buildxact and Assignar are Australian and do not lead with it. EstimateOne is the only player treating "17 years in Australian construction" as its first claim. Australian-built, Australian-run, Australian standards, Australian contract law is available, and it matters for a product whose output is a document that has to hold up locally.

## The three models worth copying, and what to take from each

### 1. Felix (felix.net) — the primary structural and tonal model
Australian, ASX-listed, enterprise procurement, four modules including tendering, two-sided, selling to a COO who signs off on governance. The most institutionally credible Australian site found, and it does the job in **seven sections**.

Take: the **subheadline that is a plain list of the modules** (`Bring vendor management, procurement activity planning, tendering and contracts together in one platform` — no metaphor, the directness is the credibility); the **problem triptych as section one** (`Data is scattered` / `Time is wasted` / `Risk is hard to see`, two unglamorous sentences each — BuilderHQ's writes itself: every builder prices a different scope, quotes arrive in incomparable formats, nobody can tell what was excluded); **four module cards, one dense paragraph each, each linking to a full page**; **"For Vendors" as a peer nav item** rather than a homepage section fighting for space (builders are BuilderHQ's equivalent); and the **testimonial format**, which is the single highest-value item in this document.

### 2. Procore (procore.com, especially /owners) — the audience architecture model
Category leader, solves exactly the three-audience problem, and the site BuilderHQ's builders already know.

Take: **"Who We Serve" as a top-level nav peer of the product** — the structural answer to three audiences, and not a toggle; **one outcome line per audience on the homepage, a full page behind each**; and critically, **the dedicated page restates the whole product in that audience's vocabulary rather than filtering features**. `/owners` is not Procore-minus-bits, it is Procore re-argued as capital deployment, with its own hero (`Capital portfolio management, one connected platform`), its own statistics (`95% Owners who say data visibility increases confidence`), its own logos and its own FAQ. BuilderHQ needs three of these: owners argue certainty of price and clarity of scope; architects argue documentation integrity and a defensible recommendation; builders argue a fair, comparable, non-wasteful bid process. Also take **publishing the unflattering review score beside the good ones**, and **per-audience proof** rather than one proof block for three audiences.

### 3. EstimateOne (estimateone.com) — the domain model, structure only
The incumbent Australian construction tendering marketplace, 17 years old, three-sided. The site BuilderHQ will be compared against, and its structure is validated by the exact market being entered.

Take: **the three roles as primary navigation**, each dropdown listing jobs-to-be-done rather than features; **all audiences addressed in sequence on one homepage** in second person after the unified story is told; **"Why BuilderHQ" as six credibility claims of which only one is a feature** (E1's are provenance, responsiveness, local presence, security, trust, one capability — copy that ratio); and **live inventory on a public page** (E1 lists real tenders with value bands and categories; BuilderHQ's privacy-safe equivalent is recent rounds showing project type, region, value band, tenders received, days from scope approval to award).

Do not take: Roboto 900 display type, the icon-and-stock-photo visual language, or the page-builder craft level. That gap is where BuilderHQ wins.

### Supporting models for two specific problems
**Voice and hero discipline: Xero (xero.com/au).** One headline, one sentence, one button, one full viewport, and the button goes to the price list. Warm, plain, second person, Australian, no urgency. Xero also shows how to serve a secondary professional audience without diluting the primary one, and how to run commercial offers without contaminating the brand: quarantine them in a dismissible bar and the pricing zone, never in the hero or the proof sections.

**The proof and trust layer: Vanta (/customers) and Docusign.** Vanta's case studies are filterable by framework, product, industry, team size and location (the filter includes ANZ), every testimonial in a rigid Name | Title | Company | Photo | Logo format with per-customer outcome statistics. Docusign's trust apparatus is the model for the page explaining what "under signature" and "every page read" actually mean. Both matter because BuilderHQ's core claim is procedural rigour, and rigour has to be documented rather than asserted.

**The numbers: Payapps and BuildPass.** Payapps for the penetration format and the fairness framing (`Making progress claims simpler, faster and fairer for everyone` — fairness is the word that makes a two-sided platform sound like infrastructure rather than a vendor). BuildPass for unrounded operational telemetry. BuilderHQ's honest equivalents: pages read, scope items produced, tenders received per round, median days from scope approval to award, percentage of scope items marked by every tenderer.

## How this bears on the brief's open decisions

- **Decision 1 (does the role morph survive):** the evidence is one-sided. No serious platform uses a role toggle; they all use navigation. This does not settle it, but it puts the burden of proof on keeping it.
- **Decision 2 (which audience leads):** Procore and Xero both lead with the volume audience and give the professional audience a peer nav destination plus one in-page doorway. Felix inverts it and leads with the governance buyer.
- **Decision 3 (the one-sentence category):** the pattern says put the plain category in the H1 and save the brand line for the subhead.
- **Decision 4 (three modules or four steps):** Felix runs four module cards, Procore names six areas and links out, Stripe names none. All three prove depth one level down.
- **Decision 6 (testimonials):** the Felix format resolves the Australian Consumer Law problem, because a testimonial built from customer-since, user count and records under management requires real facts to exist before it can be written.
- **Decision 10 (how far the type system moves):** Xero at ~60px sentence-case hero and Linear at 0.5px hairlines are the two poles. Both are quieter than the current site.
